Who are we talking about?

One term to get straight before we start.

Indigenous peoples are the original inhabitants of a particular region or country, and their descendants. They lived in these places before later groups arrived through colonisation, settlement or migration.

Indigenous peoples are not one single group. They have different languages, cultures, traditions and histories. In English-speaking countries, examples include First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples in Canada; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in Australia; Māori in New Zealand; and Native American and Alaska Native peoples in the United States.

Indigenous does not mean "a person who lived there a long time ago." Indigenous peoples are contemporary communities — with their own identities, languages and cultures today.

Health Inequality in Indigenous Communities

When we talk about health, we often think about doctors, hospitals, medicine and individual lifestyle choices. However, health is influenced by much more than what happens inside a doctor's office. Where people live, their income, education, housing, environment, culture and access to healthcare can all affect their health.

This is particularly important when we look at Indigenous communities in English-speaking countries. In several of these countries, Indigenous populations experience significant health inequalities compared with non-Indigenous populations. In Australia, First Nations people continue to experience higher rates of illness and mortality and a shorter life expectancy than non-Indigenous Australians.

In Canada, Indigenous people can face greater barriers to accessing healthcare, particularly in remote communities, alongside reported experiences of discrimination within the healthcare system. In New Zealand, Māori also experience poorer health outcomes and greater barriers to timely, high-quality care than the general population; the country's Māori Health Strategy identifies prevention, access, workforce and Māori leadership as key areas for closing that gap.

Why does the gap exist?

It would be too simple to say that Indigenous people are less healthy because of individual choices. Health inequalities are connected to social determinants of health — the conditions in which people are born, grow up, live, work and age.

One major factor is history. Colonisation changed Indigenous societies dramatically: loss of land, political power and access to traditional resources, along with discriminatory government policies, affected families, communities, languages and cultures — and these historical experiences continue to shape health today.

A second factor is geography. Remote or rural communities can find it harder to reach hospitals and specialists — and distance is not the only issue, since healthcare also needs to be culturally appropriate and respectful to be effective.

Poverty, housing, education and employment matter too. Poor housing can contribute to illness; lower income makes healthy food and transport harder to afford; limited opportunities affect long-term health.

Culture is part of the picture as well. Many Indigenous approaches to health are holistic — for example, the Māori model Te Whare Tapa Whā understands health through four interconnected dimensions: physical health, mental and emotional wellbeing, family relationships, and spiritual wellbeing.

The key idea: improving Indigenous health isn't only about individual choices. It can mean improving living conditions, reducing discrimination, increasing access to healthcare, supporting Indigenous leadership, and respecting Indigenous knowledge.

So the more useful question isn't only "why are some Indigenous communities less healthy?" — it's:

"What has happened in society that has created these inequalities, and what can be done to change them?"
